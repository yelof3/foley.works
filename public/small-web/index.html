The Small Web (and What It Means to Me)

I was welcomed to the 'small web' recently, which felt nice and led to some thoughts.

November 19, 2025

As I detailed in my about page, this static site was born from the ashes of my Wordpress website. I’ve been slowly editing it and adding additional content over the last six months or so, and it’s been a joy.

After a few weeks under the belt, I felt confident enough in the build to share the news of the new static site on my Mastodon account. Someone replied with “welcome to the small web”, and I’ve thought about that greeting on multiple occasions. Not so much the kind greeting, but the term, ‘small web’. Sounded like a nice place to be!

What is the Small Web?

I had to look into the term, and found that it describes (for the most part) small, noncommercial, personal static websites that harken back to the earlier days of the internet. Now we’re really talking! This post from 2021 by Ben Hoyt is often linked to when talking about the meaning of ’the small web’, and for good reason. The summary of the post, from Ben:

I believe that small websites are compelling aesthetically, but are also important to help us resist selling our souls to large tech companies. In this essay I present a vision for the “small web” as well as the small software and architectures that power it. Also, a bonus rant about microservices.

What the Small Web Means to Me

When I first started accessing the internet, circa 1994, it was wide open but pretty small, and very wild wild west-y. It had yet to be vastly consumerized, and unless you had the chops, it was rather difficult to create a website and put it live on the internet. I certainly didn’t have the chops back then.

Blogging

Blogging platforms and social media networks came along and allowed people with no coding experience, like me at the time, to publish things on the internet. I never joined the Myspace platform (I did have a tribe.net account, to keep in touch with Burning Man friends), but I did create a blog to showcase some of the weird and creative music I listen to. I met friends across the globe from that blog, some of whom I still keep in touch with to this day.

I happened upon a blog by Dj/Rupture, and saw that he had a friends/links section with links to his friends’ and colleagues’ website or blog links. They also maintained links sections, and they all frequently referred to each other and linked to each other’s content. Inspired, I started linking to blogs, websites, and artists who’s work I loved.

The essence of those connections is a big part of my personal concept of the ‘small web’.

Sadly, many of those bloggers reduced their posting frequency over time as the frequency of their tweets and social media posts increased. I did the same thing, and wish that we all maintained our own special place on the internet, whether that be a blog, or a small website. And I wish that we all thoughtfully linked to each other’s spots on the web.

Now you may be saying “Hey John, blogging platforms aren’t part of the small web”, and I guess I’d agree with you. But Blogspot and tribe.net weren’t the massive, evil doing comapnies that currently dominate the social landscape. They weren’t focused on sales, or data capture (as far as I know), or siloing folks under their ecosystem.

The Fediverse

Similarly to those blogs, another big part of my idea of the small web are all of the social media networks that comprise the fediverse. These are social platforms that aren’t owned by companies, and are set up and managed by regular folk like you and I. They’re filled with amazing people from all over the world, and have no ads and no algorithms. They’re pretty neat. You can learn a bit about the Fediverse here.

It bothers me that we’ve ended up in a world where most of the creation and sharing of content (not to mention personal data) happens under the umbrella of a few utterly massive, provenly nefarious tech companies.

This doesn’t have to be the case, and there is a possible future where it isn’t. It won’t be easy for us to get there, but it’s possible.

Hurdles of Joining the Small Web

Why don’t more people join this ‘small web’ Shangri La? I see a few major hurdles preventing that from happening.

Knowledge/Skills

It does take a (very learnable) set of skills to set up a personal spot on the internet that isn’t attached to a massive tech company. One of the big appeals to social media platforms is that they avoid the requirement of learning those skills altogether. But I think these are skills worth having! I personally don’t want to feel like I’m teathered to one or two companies who make their money off of my data and engagement. Especially when we’ve seen to what extent these social media companies are willing to do to capitalize on our engagement for their shareholders.

On the social media side of things, it is a bit easier to conceptualize and join a mainstream social media platform compared to opening a Mastodon account. But, not by much, and I have to assume that this is something that’s being worked on.

The Word

It’s tough spreading the word about small web alternatives without a marketing budget! Often times these alternatives are non-profit organizations or otherwise collections of regular folk joining together to build a great platform. So the marketing dollars are slim to none, and of course that’s tough to bring to an advertising marketplace where Facebook/Meta, for example, is spending billions annually on advertising.

Apathy

I’ll be the first to admit, this little passion of mine is ‘a weird hill to die on’. Most people, including all of my local friends, really don’t care about distancing themselves from tech giants. And I’m not blaming them- these platforms do the trick for them, and it’s where people are. Nor do they care about static websites. I bet they think I’m at best weird, and at worst anti-social for caring about these things.

There’s also apathy with younger internet users. When I talk to my daughter about having her own, non tech giant place on the web for herself, she looks at my like I have three heads. My guess is that I’d likely get the same reaction from anyone under 45.

F.O.M.O.

The fear of missing out is a real thing. I felt a bit of it when I stopped using Facebook and Instagram, and I know that probably exists for most of the users on those platforms and others. Big social media platforms are where most people are posting, so there is an appeal to be present in those spaces. I’ve likely missed a few social event notifications from friends who only use Instagram for their social media.

Lack of Diversity

Except for the fascists and racists among us, I think most of us want our social media platforms to represent our actual communities, which are comprised of lots of different types of folk.

As it currently exists, there is a diversity problem on Mastodon. It tends to attract progressive, older, more tech savvy users, with a majority of those being white and male, and a decent chunk of people coming from Europe.

That said, nobody that I’ve seen on the platform wants any sort of lack of diversity, and the issue is talked about openly. While there might be certain demographic buckets that are filled more than others, the platform and community does work hard at inclusivity, and caring for others. Whether it be content warnings, or safe spaces for LGBTQIA folk, furry folk, or autistic folk, etc, the community overall is very inclusive to people of all walks of life.

Benefits of Joining the Small Web

I see many benefits to all of us developing a space on the internet that we control, and that isn’t affiliated with a big company desperate for our data. Here are a few things I appreciate about the small web.

Speed

Site speed: I knew this new static site would be quicker than my old Wordpress site, but I had no idea it would be this much quicker. I’ve even slowed it down with a little javascript for audio and video, and it still smokes my last iteration of the site on Wordpress.

Security

This site is much more secure than my old Wordpress installation, which relied on a few plugins. I’ve always been careful with which plugins I use, but after my site’s host lagged with php updates, I got a little sloppy and downloaded a plugin designed to revert websites back to previous Wordpress versions. While it technically did the trick, it also created hundreds of sales pages on my website.

There’s none of that with this static site, primarily because it doesn’t run any server-side code, but also because there are no forms, publically accessable login pages, etc. The simplicity of the site reduces the overall attack surface of the site to a would-be malicious actor.

Price

It turns out that saving our souls from the big tech companies isn’t that expensive! This site for example, being deployed through GitHub, is free to host, so I just have my annual domain renewal fee.

Conclusion

It’ll always be an uphill battle, but I’d love to convince a friend or two to come over to the small web. I think they’d be very happy.

It’s not for everyone. On the website end of things, I’m not building out a static site for a client or friend if they need an ecommerce site with more complex form integrations and an ecommerce platform integration like Shopify. But I would recommend static websites for anyone who’s looking to set up a smaller, rather basic personal site like the one that you’re on now. Minimal coding is needed, and I think the benefits far outweigh the disadvantages.

On the social media side of things, I personally think that everyone should be using social media platforms built with the ActivityPub protocol, like Mastodon, Pixelfed, etc. Places that make up the fediverse. I truly believe that the only people who wouldn’t love it are people who really enjoy getting advertisements and unrequested viewbait on their timelines, and who don’t mind being under a corporate umbrella.
